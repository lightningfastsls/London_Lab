Does the fine structure of the gap between USVs carry meaning — and have we preserved it?

For Mickey · 2026-06-04 · context: WS-B grammar analysis (timing dominates) · Shachar + Claude

Shachar's observation. When two USVs follow each other, the length of the silent gap between them might mean different things at a fine scale — a very short gap vs a longer one could be two different behaviours, not just "faster vs slower." This is separate from a within-call frequency jump (a step inside one syllable), which our shape analysis already treats as its own category — i.e. the zero-gap case is accounted for. The open question is the non-zero gaps. (The specific "3 ms vs 10 ms" earlier were just illustrative values — the question is about fine gap structure in general, not those numbers.)

The short gaps essentially don't occur. There is a hard floor: almost nothing below ~25 ms, and the mass sits at 50–100 ms (median ~78 ms) in both cohorts. The two distributions are nearly identical (wild vs lab).

2. Can we even see fine gaps? (resolution vs segmentation)

Two different "resolutions" matter, and they disagree:

  • Spectrogram resolution: STFT hop = 128 samples @ 300 kHz = 0.43 ms/frame. We could, in principle, resolve a few-ms difference easily.
  • Segmentation resolution: our detector deliberately collapses short gaps. The energy detector merges detections < 3 ms apart (merge_gap_ms = 3.0) and bridges gaps up to 5 ms (segment_continuity_max_gap_ms = 5.0), with the explicit goal — quoting our own config — of "bridging energy dips within single USVs while splitting multi-syllable calls."

So our pipeline draws the "one call vs two calls" line at ~3–5 ms, by a tuning choice. But that only explains the absence of sub-5 ms gaps — it does not explain the empty 5–25 ms region. That region is either real biology (mice don't re-initiate a call that fast) or an artifact of a different stage. We can't tell from the data alone.

3. Why this matters for the grammar result

WS-B found that the within-bout sequence "grammar" is carried predominantly by timing, not by syllable shape-order. But that timing signal lives on the 50–250 ms rhythm scale (where the gaps actually are) — it is not a statement about fine sub-syllable timing, because that scale is mostly empty in our data. This is consistent with Perrodin et al. 2023 (females track rhythm), but it leaves Shachar's question open: if there is meaningful fine-gap structure, our current segmentation may be averaging over it or merging it away.

4. Questions for Mickey

Q1 — biology of the boundary. Is there a real behavioural distinction between (a) two separate USVs with a very short silence and (b) a single USV with an internal frequency step (a "jump")? Where, in milliseconds, would you draw the line between "one call" and "two calls"? Our code currently splits at ~3–5 ms.
Q2 — the empty <25 ms region. Does the near-absence of gaps below ~25 ms match what you'd expect physiologically (a minimum re-initiation / refractory time between calls)? Or does it look like we might be over-merging closely-spaced calls into one?
Q3 — continuous rhythm vs discrete categories. For the grammar analysis, should the gap be treated as a continuous quantity (rhythm/tempo, as we did), or do you expect discrete gap categories — e.g. "tightly linked" vs "loosely sequenced" calls — that we should be modelling as distinct states?
Q4 — where to look. If fine-gap structure is biologically meaningful, what scale should we examine — modes on a log-gap axis? a specific sub-100 ms band? — and is there a known behavioural correlate (arousal, bout onset, partner response) we should align it to?
